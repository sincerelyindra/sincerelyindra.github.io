Revenue management / Robust optimization / Reproduction study

Robust pricing with minimal demand information

How should a seller set one price when the demand curve is largely unknown—and the few observed purchase probabilities are noisy?

This project reproduces the core computational structure of robust quantile pricing under α-regular demand, then pushes it into a historical retail backtest and a feasibility-aware confidence-interval extension. The emphasis is deliberately computational: preserve the source theory where it is implementable, state the numerical approximation where it is not, and test whether the resulting method remains useful outside a clean simulation. 01

The research question

Classical pricing assumes a demand curve. Here, the seller sees only a few prices and uncertain purchase probabilities—and must still choose a price with a worst-case guarantee.

Let F̄(p)=1−F(p) be the survival probability that a customer valuation exceeds price p. With market size normalized to one, revenue is R(p)=pF̄(p). The difficulty is that F̄ is not observed. Instead, at a small set of prices w₁,…,wK, the seller has exact or interval-valued quantile information. The ambiguity set is restricted by α-regularity, which includes classical regularity at α=0 and the monotone-hazard-rate condition at α=1. [R:§3] [G]

Uncertain quantile observation
F̄(wᵢ) ∈ [qᵢᴸ, qᵢᴴ],   i = 1,…,K

The interval form acknowledges sampling noise instead of treating a small-sample estimate as exact.

Worst-case competitive ratio
CR(p,𝓕α) = infF∈𝓕α   pF̄(p) / maxv≥0 vF̄(v)

The denominator is the revenue a clairvoyant seller would obtain if the true distribution were known.

02

Why the infinite-dimensional problem becomes tractable

The key structural idea is geometric. Pointwise-valid confidence intervals may still be mutually incompatible with α-regularity. Generalized Pareto interpolation is used to tighten those raw intervals into globally feasible bounds without changing the ambiguity set. The same interpolation geometry then constructs extremal lower and upper envelopes for the adversarial distribution. [R:§4.1] [G]

Generalized Pareto tail
Γα(v) = (1+(1−α)v)−1/(1−α) for α<1,   and   Γ1(v)=e−v

This is the coordinate system used by the interpolation and envelope construction.

Feasibility test after tightening
𝓕α ≠ ∅   ⇔   qᴸ ≤ qᴴ   coordinatewise

After applying all shape-implied restrictions, feasibility reduces to checking whether each tightened lower bound is below its upper bound.

Nature’s reduced problem
infF∈𝓕α CR(p,F)   →   infr≥0 CR(p,F*r)

The source theory shows that Nature need only choose a scalar monopoly pivot r; the extremal curve is then assembled from the flattest feasible left tail and steepest feasible right tail at the maximal feasible pivot height.

α = 0 · regular distributions

Recovers the classical Myerson regularity condition through monotonic virtual value.

α = 1 · MHR

Imposes the stronger monotone-hazard-rate structure; the generalized Pareto tail becomes exponential.

03

Executable solver architecture

The implementation follows the source paper’s objects, but the final reduced adversarial search is solved numerically on dense grids rather than by the appendix-level closed-form cubic routines.

Important caveat

The project is a faithful reproduction of the framework and a best-effort reproduction of the exact algorithm. The source paper’s appendix-level closed-form O(K²) / cubic-equation subroutines are not reimplemented; dense-grid numerical optimization is used instead. This distinction is central to interpreting the mixed synthetic benchmark. [R:§5.1,§9.2]

04

Three experimental tracks

Part A · Reproduction

Does uncertainty preserve feasibility?

Compare exact plug-in quantiles with Wilson intervals on four benchmark distributions; then compare robust pricing with linear, quadratic, and logit proxies under simulated demand.

Part B · Historical test

Does it survive real pricing history?

Run a rolling backtest on canned-tuna scanner data using only past information, and score robust pricing against ERM and three parametric demand proxies.

Part C · Extension

Can interval width adapt to feasibility?

Compare fixed 90%, 95%, and 99% confidence levels with a narrowest-feasible rule and a validation-guided rule across α∈{0,1}.

05

Reproduction: feasibility succeeds, synthetic performance is mixed

The strongest reproduced result is feasibility under small samples. With n=10 Bernoulli observations at each of three support-relative prices, exact plug-in quantiles frequently violate the global shape restrictions. Wilson intervals remain feasible in essentially every trial. [R:§7.1]

06

Historical rolling backtest

The strongest empirical result in the project is not the clean simulation—it is the noisier historical test.

Across 40 eligible store–UPC pairs and 1,508 rolling windows, robust pricing attains the highest mean revenue ratio and the strongest worst-decile performance. The median ratio is 1.000 for every method, so the separation appears mainly in difficult windows rather than easy or tied ones. [R:§7.3]

The average improvement is modest but persistent; the tail improvement is larger. That pattern is consistent with the purpose of a robust method: its practical value appears most clearly when the window is hard and model error matters more.

Why evaluation uses the nearest observed future price

Scanner data do not reveal counterfactual demand at prices that were never posted in the test window. Evaluating a recommendation at an unsupported future price would require another demand model and would turn the historical test back into a simulation. The project therefore maps each recommended price to the nearest price actually observed in the six-week test window. [R:§8.2]

07

Extension: adapt confidence intervals to feasibility

A fixed 99% interval is safe but often wide; a fixed 90% interval is sharper but may produce an empty ambiguity set. The project’s main extension uses a simple monotonicity fact—widening intervals cannot destroy an already-feasible distribution—to select the narrowest confidence level that preserves feasibility. [R:§5.4,§8.1]

Try 90%If the tightened ambiguity set is feasible, stop.
→
Else try 95%Widen only when the narrower interval fails.
→
Else use 99%The widest candidate acts as the fallback.

For α=0, AdaptiveFeasible reaches mean ratio 0.839 with full feasibility and mean interval width 0.239, versus 0.818 and width 0.372 for fixed 99%. For α=1, both adaptive rules achieve mean ratio 0.847; fixed 99% reaches 0.841. More strongly, the report states that AdaptiveFeasible weakly dominates every fixed confidence level in every (model, n) cell evaluated for both values of α. [R:§7.4]

08

What the project establishes—and what it does not

Supported by the implementation and experiments

  • Interval-valued quantiles are dramatically more stable than exact plug-in quantiles under the project’s small-sample feasibility experiment.
  • The executable solver captures generalized Pareto interpolation, interval tightening, envelope construction, and the reduced robust-price search.
  • The robust method leads the compared baselines in the 1,508-window historical backtest, especially in lower-tail performance.
  • Feasibility-aware interval calibration improves or matches fixed confidence-level rules in the reported adaptive-CI study.

Limitations kept explicit

  • The solver is a dense-grid approximation, not a theorem-by-theorem implementation of the source paper’s closed-form appendix routines.
  • The synthetic benchmark does not uniformly reproduce the source paper’s headline superiority.
  • The historical dataset does not expose true conversion probabilities; the backtest uses a clipped quantity-based proxy.
  • Nearest-observed-price evaluation is historically defensible but does not measure fully counterfactual revenue at unposted prices.
